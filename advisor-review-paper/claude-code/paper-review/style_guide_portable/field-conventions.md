# Field conventions

This file describes how five robotics and HRI dissertations are written. It shows what experienced readers in the field have done in their own long documents, and so what they will find familiar in a paper or thesis. The patterns are observations, not rules. Where they differ from the [writing style guide](writing-style-guide.md), the guide takes priority. The source quotations were removed to keep the file small. Measures are approximate and come from the running prose of the numbered chapters.

The first three are called the recent writers below, and Matarić and Scassellati the earlier writers. The earlier two show which habits are older conventions of robotics dissertations.

## Shared practices

These are the practices that the three recent writers share.

1. State the thesis in one sentence and repeat that sentence in the abstract, the introduction, and the conclusion.
2. Name the gap precisely. Say what prior work covers, what it leaves out, and why.
3. Show a problem in a short scene before stating it in general terms.
4. Open each chapter with what it presents and what was found, and say how it follows from the chapter before.
5. Report every study in the same order, with questions or hypotheses first and the verdict attached to the result that decides it.
6. Give a result with its values and its test in one sentence, and keep the interpretation in a hedged clause after it.
7. Report a difference that was not found as not found. Do not call the conditions equal.
8. Offer explanations for an unexpected result as possibilities.
9. Grade the strength of evidence in words, such as preliminary, limited, some support, and a trend, with the value beside the word.
10. State limitations plainly and tie each one to the design of the study.
11. Derive future work from the scope and limits of the work, with a modal that shows how firm the plan is.
12. Define each term in a full sentence when it first matters, and say what is outside the scope.
13. End each chapter with what it showed and what that provides for the next one.
14. Keep the final chapter short, at three to four pages, and close on what the work is for.
15. Say where each chapter was published and who contributed, and give the introduction one figure of the whole approach.

The earlier two share most of items 2 to 4 and 10 to 13. Items 5 to 9 concern user studies with hypotheses and statistical tests, which the earlier two, both of which build and evaluate systems, have few of.

## The writers in brief

- **Short.** Formal, and impersonal in the parts that frame the work. The longest sentences, often carrying a result with its condition and its interpretation. Each contribution moves through the same verbs, formalized, developed, instantiated, and validated. A chapter opens with a short abstract and ends with a Summary. Claims are graded with some support, limited evidence, and preliminary. Frequent openers are Additionally, However, and Finally.
- **Vázquez.** Direct and orderly, with we throughout. Short sentences and the fewest colons and semicolons. General statements are followed by For example, and reasons and limits are counted with First, Second, and Third. Discussion paragraphs open with a lead sentence that states the point. Frequent openers are For example, However, In general, and In particular.
- **Bobu.** Argumentative and vivid, with we throughout and I in the final chapter. One household example runs through most chapters. The approach is set against the usual one with rather than, instead, and by contrast. Limits are stated at length. Frequent openers are However, Unfortunately, Lastly, and For example.
- **Matarić.** Formal and mostly impersonal, with this thesis and this work as the subject. The introduction summarizes the approach and results before an outline that also gives reading paths, and a glossary defines the terms. Every behavior is evaluated against the same four named criteria. Frequent openers are However, For example, Thus, and Furthermore.
- **Scassellati.** Formal and mostly impersonal. Each chapter opens with an epigraph. The introduction moves from a grand challenge to a specific one and states plainly what the work does not claim. Frequent openers are For example, However, and Furthermore.

## Where they differ

- **First person.** We, our, and us range from 3.0 to 25.7 per 1,000 words. Even the most impersonal writers keep we in study sections. The style guide chooses the voice by genre.
- **Sentence length.** The mean ranges from 20.3 to 26.1 words. Short writes long sentences that carry a result with its conditions, and Vázquez breaks the same content into two or three sentences.
- **Colons and semicolons.** All use more than the style guide wants, which takes priority.
- **Register.** Short and Vázquez are restrained. Bobu uses strong evaluative words, contractions, and idioms. The style guide follows the restrained register.
- **Where related work sits.** Short and Matarić have one background chapter, Vázquez adds short sections before each experiment, and Bobu has a section in each chapter.
- **How a chapter ends.** Short and the earlier writers end with a Summary, and Bobu ends with a Discussion.
- **Where contributions are listed.** Short and Vázquez list them for the whole work, and Bobu lists them in each chapter.

## Conventions by kind

Patterns marked *adds* cover ground the style guide does not. Patterns marked *differs* conflict with the style guide, which wins. Unmarked patterns agree with the guide.

### Vocabulary

- **Names for the document.** Each writer settles on one or two names, such as this dissertation and this work, and repeats them. None calls the document a paper. Choose dissertation or thesis and use only that one.
- **Verbs for what a chapter does.** A chapter is the subject of presents, introduces, describes, reviews, and discusses. The verb says what follows, so reviews announces literature, introduces announces a new method, and presents announces a study or its results.
- **A fixed chain of verbs.** A model is formalized, an algorithm is developed from it, instantiated for one task, and validated or evaluated in a study. The same verbs return in every opening and summary, so the reader always knows which step a sentence reports.
- **Enable and leverage.** Enable takes an agent and an action, as in enables robots to detect. Leverage is rarer and means using something that already exists.
- **Insight and intuition.** The central idea is named before it is implemented, as in the main insight is that or the intuition is that. The sentence is short enough to remember and is reused.
- **People.** Participants inside a study. Outside a study, users, people, or end-users. Each study states the number of participants, how they were recruited, and the limits of the sample.
- **Imperfect input.** Non-expert input is called noisy or imperfect and is described as input that still carries signal. A claim about it is measured against two reference points, an expert and a random baseline.
- **Graded words for evidence.** Some support, limited evidence, preliminary evidence, a trend towards, and less conclusive sit next to the number. Demonstrate and show are kept for results with a significant test or a clear margin.
- **Hedges.** Suggest, may, might, and could each appear between 0.2 and 1.9 times per 1,000 words. Short prefers might and suggesting that, Vázquez could and tend to, and Bobu may.
- **Prior work.** Prior work is the usual phrase. A sentence about it says what the work focused on or assumed, so the next sentence can say what it leaves open.
- **Evaluative words** *(differs)*. Short is restrained. Bobu writes notoriously challenging, sorely needed, and Unfortunately. A strong word is usually attached to a problem or a need, not to the writer's own result.

### Wording of claims

- **Voice** *(differs)*. Short frames the work impersonally and uses we inside study sections. Vázquez and Bobu use we throughout. The earlier two are mostly impersonal.
- **A result with its test.** One sentence names the measure, gives the values with their spread in parentheses, and gives the test with its statistic and p value. The verb is plain, such as showed, revealed, or was significant.
- **A difference that was not found.** No significant difference was found, or this trend did not reach statistical significance. Bobu adds what the result does not mean, as in This does not mean that [A] is as good as [B].
- **Hypotheses and verdicts.** Numbered and stated before the results. The verdict is attached to the deciding result, as in supporting H1, contrary to H4, or (H6 unsupported). Partial support is said in words.
- **Possible explanations.** This may have been because, could be attributed to, an alternative explanation is that, and it is unclear whether [A] or [B].
- **Limitations stated plainly.** The study was limited in several ways. First … Second … The limit is tied to the design, with no apology. A caution names the population, as in all participants had [property], so the results speak to that population.
- **Future work.** Further research is needed to determine whether, future work could, and one possible direction is. A direction comes with what it would make possible.
- **Contributions.** A list with a lead-in sentence ending in a colon. Short separates primary from secondary contributions, Vázquez gives two take-home messages first, and Bobu starts each item with a verb.
- **Definitions.** A term is defined in its own sentence when it first matters, as in [term] is defined in this work as, and then used without variation.
- **Scope** *(adds)*. Early and in one or two sentences, as in the scope of this dissertation is limited to, or [topic] is not directly addressed in this work. Scassellati states what his robot model does not claim about humans.
- **Publication and credit.** Each chapter says where it appeared and with whom. When data came from others, one sentence says what they did and another says what was done as part of this work.
- **Figures and references** *(adds)*. The introduction has one figure of the whole approach. A result often ends with its figure in parentheses, and other parts are cited by number.

### Writing style

- **A concrete scene first.** One to three sentences, such as two people talking near a robot, before the general statement it illustrates.
- **One example carried through** *(adds)*. Bobu returns to one household scene in most chapters and changes one detail at a time. Within one paper, the paper's own task can serve this way.
- **The gap, then the response.** What existing work covers, what it leaves out, and a reason for the gap. Then Instead, and the alternative. Prior work is described without dismissal.
- **The thesis in one sentence** *(adds)*. Set apart from the paragraph and repeated word for word.
- **Research as iteration.** Studying people, building a capability, and evaluating it form a cycle. Each study informs, motivates, or enables the next, and a limit found in one study is named as the reason for the next chapter.
- **Implications for other researchers.** A discussion says what the result means for people who build or study similar systems, conditional on a goal or setting.
- **Observations and participants' words.** Numbers come with what was seen and short attributed remarks that support a point the numbers already make.
- **Candor about costs** *(adds)*. Each writer says plainly when something did not work or came at a cost, then what can still be concluded, as in [benefit] did come at a cost, since [measured cost].
- **A step, not an end point.** The size of the contribution is stated modestly, as in a step towards [goal], and paired with a clear statement of what was shown.
- **Register** *(differs)*. Formal throughout for Short, Vázquez, and the earlier two. Bobu mixes in contractions, idioms, and questions to the reader.
- **Ending on the wider stake** *(adds)*. The last paragraph leaves the technical content and says what the work is for. Short ends on an ethical concern of her field and how her approach answers it. The closing is short and is not a summary.

### Sentence style

- **Sentence length.** Long sentences carry a result with its conditions and interpretation. A short sentence is placed where a point has to stand out.
- **A short sentence that states the point.** A paragraph opens with a short claim or finding, so a discussion can be read from its first sentences alone. Vázquez sets these in small capitals.
- **Openers that mark the step.** However is the most common opener for all three recent writers. The last item of a series takes Finally or Lastly.
- **Colons and semicolons** *(differs)*. A colon introduces a list, an explanation, or an example, and a semicolon joins two parallel statements. Vázquez is the most sparing.
- **Parentheses.** Statistics, figure and section references, hypothesis labels, and a short gloss on a measure. The sentence reads correctly without them.
- **Questions** *(adds)*. Research questions as a numbered list. Vázquez and Bobu sometimes put the problem to the reader as a question where the motivation turns into the approach.
- **For example after a general claim.** One case, one sentence, naming the actor and what happens. It is the most frequent opener for Vázquez and Scassellati.
- **Tense by section** *(adds)*. Present for an overview and for what a method does, past for a procedure and a result, present for an interpretation, and past for a summary.

### Sentence structure

- **A result with its interpreting clause.** The result is the main clause and is not hedged. A clause beginning suggesting that, supporting, indicating, or which means carries the hedged interpretation.
- **Concession first.** While, Although, or Even though opens the sentence with the granted point, and the main clause follows.
- **Rather than and instead.** The approach is set against the usual one inside the sentence, or with By contrast at the start of the next.
- **First, Second, Third.** The number is announced, as in the motivation was twofold, then each item opens with First and Second.
- **Purpose before the action.** To or In order to opens the sentence, so each step reads as an answer to a need.
- **This with a noun.** This approach, this limitation, these insights. The noun classifies what was just said.
- **Parallel sentences for conditions.** In the former case … In the latter … The same shape lets the reader compare what differs.
- **The more, the more** *(adds)*. A relationship between two quantities stated as a proportion, with the statistic when it was measured.
- **Because at the start.** A reason that governs the whole sentence comes first, often for a design decision.

### Structure

- **The introduction.** All three reach what the work does within the first two pages. Vázquez runs need, scene, gap, goal, insight, challenges, scope, and outline. Bobu runs failures, question, example, position, core insight, and parts. Short runs definition, what is presented, domain, approach figure, contributions, and outline.
- **An outline with content.** One sentence or a short paragraph for each chapter, with its question and main result, so the argument can be read on one page.
- **Chapter openings.** A short statement of what the chapter presents and found, then a paragraph that places it after the earlier chapters.
- **Linking chapters.** The results of the previous chapter support a claim, and this chapter builds on them. Or a limit of the last study motivated this one.
- **Chapter endings.** A Summary in the past tense that restates the method, the study, and the result, then what the results support. Or a Discussion that ends with a sentence beginning Overall.
- **A study in a fixed order.** Questions or hypotheses, design, measures, participants, results grouped by measure, and discussion, with the same headings for every study.
- **A method from idea to steps.** The idea in words, then why the direct approach is difficult, then the steps with First and Then, and only then the symbols or algorithm.
- **Related work that ends on this work.** Each group of studies ends with what remains unclear or with By contrast and the specific difference.
- **A discussion in themed paragraphs.** Each theme is visible at the start of its paragraph, through a run-in heading or a lead sentence. Themes cover what was found, what it means for design, what limits it, and what comes next.
- **Discussion order.** Restate the goal, say which hypotheses held, explain results that went against expectation, state the limits, and end with what the study provides next.
- **Future work from scope and limits.** A number of choices were made in scoping this work, and each provides a starting point. Each direction starts from what the work did and says what would change.
- **The final chapter** *(adds)*. Three to four pages, with a recap, the contributions in the past tense, future directions, and a short closing. None of the three has a separate chapter that discusses findings across chapters. Take-home messages are an option.
- **Reading paths and a glossary** *(adds, Matarić only)*. The outline tells readers which chapters to skip to by interest, and an appendix defines recurring terms.
- **Epigraphs** *(adds, Scassellati only)*. A quotation before each chapter, with author, year, and page. A personal choice that the recent writers do not make.
